## 2. Core Content

### 2.1 Where can the money come from — five starting points
| Source | Examples |
|---|---|
| Own funds | Savings, retained profits |
| Customers | Sales revenue, pre-orders & paid pilots |
| Grants | Public programmes, foundations |
| Debt | Loans & credit, leasing |
| Equity | Business angels, VC & strategic investors |

Plus: crowdfunding (pre-sales/loans/equity depending on model) and accelerators/networks (support and investor access, sometimes funding).
**Key line: "Your funding plan can combine several sources."**

### 2.2 Match the capital to the job
| Need | Business profile | Options to explore | Key question |
|---|---|---|---|
| Buy equipment | Established, predictable cash flow | Retained profits, leasing/loan | Can the business afford repayments? |
| **Validate technology** | **Early-stage, unproven technology** | **Eligible grants, angel/strategic investment** | **What technical evidence will the funding create?** |
| Expand sales | Paying customers entering a new market | Customer cash/debt, equity for growth | How predictable are sales, how fast must we grow? |

### 2.3 Angels vs. VC funds
- **Business angel:** invests own money, personal goals, individual/syndicate decisions, may bring expertise/contacts.
- **VC fund:** manages investors' capital, defined mandate, portfolio/ownership targets, fund returns/exit expectations.
- Why size matters: "Some investments fail. A few large successes may need to drive the fund's returns." Example: a €100m fund owning 10% at a €100m exit receives €10m (simplified, pre-fees/tax).

### 2.4 A good business may not fit venture capital
Two illustrative companies: an established, profitable €2m-revenue consultancy wanting steady growth (poor VC fit) vs. an early-stage, loss-making €200k-revenue software company aiming for rapid global scale (fits the VC model). **"Business quality and investor fit are different questions."**

### 2.5 Every type of money has a price
| Funding | Cost / commitment |
|---|---|
| Own funds | Capital at risk, opportunity cost |
| Customer money | Delivery, quality, customer commitments |
| Grants | Eligibility, restricted use, reporting, time |
| Debt | Repayment, interest, possible security/guarantees |
| Equity | Ownership, future upside, shared influence |

### 2.6 How investor interest becomes investment
TARGET → MEET → VERIFY → AGREE TERMS → CLOSE. **"A positive meeting is not a funding commitment."**

### 2.7 What investors assess — DanBAN (Danish Business Angels) 5 screening areas
Team · Traction · Scalability · Market · Terms — *"Prepare evidence for each answer."* (Source: danban.org/startups)

### 2.8 What should the money help you achieve
Distinguish a **spending plan** ("hire a salesperson") from a **business milestone** ("win 10 paying customers in Germany within 12 months") — the milestone is what actually gets tested.

### 2.9 Live Funding Clinic — 60-second pitch format
1. Your milestone — what are you trying to achieve?
2. Your funding ask — how much, from which proposed sources?
3. Your question — what do you need help deciding?

### 2.10 Equity crowdfunding's real legal limit in Denmark
Verbatim from Narcis: crowdfunding via equity can go to a **maximum of 150 investors** (below ~€50m raised) before triggering wealth-management regulation. Practical framing he gave: divide the amount you need by 150 to see the per-person ask — e.g., raising 1M DKK from 150 people instead of one investor for the same amount.

### 2.11 Why NGOs don't fit this session's topic
Narcis, directly: NGOs run on public funding/donations/foundations, not private equity — "you don't own your NGO," so there's no equity to exchange. Foundations are the closest thing to "private" money NGOs can access, and even those are typically funded by wealthy individuals' estates after death, not living investors seeking returns.

### 2.12 A pointed line worth keeping, on AI and grant applications
Narcis, on why precision matters more now, not less: *"In the world of AI, where you can generate applications in minutes, what will win is evidence-based precise ask."* His reasoning: evaluators facing 1,000+ AI-assisted applications (versus 50 before) now pre-filter with AI themselves before a human ever reads it — so an application has to survive that filter first.

## 3. Applied to FixProve

### 3.1 "Would you sell 20% of your business to fund its next step?" — conclusion reached
**No, not now.** Zero price-discovery yet — no paying customer, no D3 evidence (window runs to 2026-11-12). Selling equity before revenue prices FixProve on a pitch, not proof — the same 20% is worth more after even one paying customer and a renewal. ### His actual case study (numbers, verified from the transcript)
- **Pre-seed round:** raised 1.25M DKK for 10% equity from 2 Danish business angels, after ~250 cold outreach emails/LinkedIn messages. His own mental benchmark for outreach volume: "think 1,000 — if you hit it ahead of time, great; if not, you're only halfway."
- **Seed round:** 15M DKK from 5 investors, ~6–7 months start to close (his stated "pretty typical" timeline). Originally aimed for 25M but had to cut the round after two investors got cold feet during closing — a lesson he named explicitly: **always try to get oversubscribed** (more committed than you need) so you hold the leverage to choose, rather than being desperate at the finish line.
- **Grants alongside equity:** an Innobooster-style grant (~800-900K DKK), a larger ~5M DKK grant, plus several smaller ones — used *in combination* with the equity rounds, not instead of them.
- **Non-sales evidence used before any revenue existed:** signed (non-binding) letters of interest from major industrial customers (named: Vestas, Ørsted, a construction firm), plus in-kind hours/testing support from those same companies. His framing: *"If you have sales, that's the best evidence — but if you don't, find other formalized ways."*
- **Terms red flags he named directly:** a 1x liquidation preference is standard; **2x or higher is a real red flag**. One investor demanded a 90% shareholder-vote threshold for major decisions (talked down to 80%, "still silly") — watch for non-standard voting/veto terms slipped in once the headline percentage is agreed.
- **The failure, told plainly:** Airflight's board advised outsourcing fundraising to an external firm; the firm reported false progress for months, then admitted at the worst moment they had nothing — triggering an emergency when a bridge loan fell through. The company burned down from 17 to 2 employees and eventually closed (March 2026). What survived it: radical transparency with investors throughout — weekly written updates including the bad news, not just the good. His own line: *"Telling them when things are great doesn't build trust. Telling them when things are bad does."*
- **On LinkedIn/investor networking (his direct advice, worth applying before the call):** search "investor" on LinkedIn, save profiles to a spreadsheet, send a genuine, specific connection note referencing something from their profile before ever mentioning funding. Post about your industry roughly weekly — not to be read in depth, but so a prospective investor's first look at your profile shows an established pattern, not a blank page.
- **On rejection:** treat early "no"s as calibration — pitch less-critical investors first to refine delivery before approaching top targets; a well-handled rejection ("come back when you've solved X, Y, Z") is a real, re-openable door months later.
